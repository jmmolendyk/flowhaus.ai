Progressive AI Governance

The right governance, at the right level.

Governance is part of successful AI implementation. The racetrack model gives it a shape: simple rules where speed is safe, engineered safeguards where consequence rises, and precise controls where the stakes are highest.

Our philosophy

Governance is part of successful AI implementation.

Organizations are gaining the ability to develop and deploy AI applications faster than ever before. Employees can build their own tools, departments can automate complex processes, and new business capabilities can emerge without traditional development cycles.

But the ability to build software quickly does not remove the organization’s responsibility for what that software does, the information it processes, or the business operations that come to depend on it.

Effective governance helps organizations make better decisions throughout the implementation lifecycle.

It helps establish whether an application addresses the right business problem, whether it has been designed and evaluated appropriately, and whether the organization can maintain and monitor it over time.

As applications become more consequential, governance provides the additional structure necessary to pursue opportunities that might otherwise be too risky.

The objective is not to govern every application in the same way. It is to apply the right governance where it creates value.

  • Too little

    Tools nobody inventoried, applications with no owner, and consequences nobody planned for.

  • The right level

    Rigor in proportion to consequence, so people move quickly where it is safe and carefully where it matters.

  • Too much

    One heavy process for every use. It slows the low-risk work and gets worked around.

Methodology

The racetrack as a governance model.

A track is built from different kinds of sections, and each is engineered for the speed it carries. Straightaways are open. Banked turns add engineered safety. Technical sections demand precision. The infield supports all of it.

Stage 1 · The straightaways

Foundational Governance

Enable responsible participation

Foundational governance establishes clear rules, approved tools, training, and boundaries. Most people can move quickly without needing individual approvals for ordinary activities.

Principle: Enable broad participation with simple, understandable rules.

Core capabilities
Approved tools and environments, acceptable use, data-handling boundaries, AI literacy, initial inventories, ownership and escalation.
Business value
Employees can participate with clearer expectations, less uncertainty and greater confidence.
Illustrative uses
Drafting and summarizing with approved tools, research, and meeting notes.

Stage 2 · The banked turns

Managed AI

Enable successful implementation

More consequential AI use requires stronger engineering, testing, monitoring, and oversight. Like banking, barriers, and vehicle safety systems, these controls allow higher performance under more demanding conditions.

Principle: Greater capability requires appropriately engineered safeguards.

Core capabilities
Use-case assessment, risk evaluation, data architecture, processor assessment, performance benchmarks, human oversight, testing, ownership and lifecycle management.
Business value
Better implementation decisions, reduced avoidable rework, measurable performance and more dependable operations.
Illustrative uses
An assistant over company documents, automation that handles customer or employee data, or an internally built application others depend on.

Stage 3 · The technical sections

Assured AI

Enable consequential opportunities

Regulated, high-consequence, or especially complex applications require precise controls, documentation, specialized expertise, and evidence of compliance.

This is where organizations that understand the requirements can gain an advantage over competitors who cannot navigate the complexity.

Principle: Precision and discipline create opportunities that speed alone cannot.

Core capabilities
Formal verification, regulatory alignment, decision traceability, documented evidence, auditability, operational resilience and change controls.
Business value
The ability to pursue more complex AI opportunities with appropriate evidence, accountability and confidence.
Illustrative uses
Decisions about hiring, lending, or patient care, regulated products, and agents acting inside regulated processes.

Organizational AI Management

An AI management system, potentially aligned with or certified to ISO/IEC 42001, provides organizational accountability, policy, risk management, oversight and continuous improvement across applicable AI activities within its defined scope. See ISO/IEC 42001 implementation.

Continuous evaluation and adaptation

Choosing the level

Consequence decides how much governance an application needs.

The levels apply to individual uses of AI, not to the organization as a whole. Five considerations help decide where a given application belongs, and a change in any of them can move it up or down.

  • Consequence

    What happens, and to whom, if the application is wrong or unavailable.

  • Data

    What information it touches, where that information goes, and what records remain under your control.

  • Autonomy

    Whether it suggests, or acts. The more it does without a person in the loop, the more the controls matter.

  • Obligations

    Regulation, contracts, and customer or sector expectations that apply to this use.

  • Scale

    How many people, processes, and decisions come to depend on it.

Business outcomes

The value of getting governance right.

The measure of successful AI adoption is not the number of applications an organization builds or the speed at which it produces prototypes. It is the value those applications create and the organization’s ability to sustain that value.

Progressive AI Governance supports five important outcomes.

  1. Build the right thing.

    Connect AI investments to business needs, process improvements, and measurable outcomes.

  2. Build it correctly.

    Address architectural, data, performance, and oversight requirements before they become expensive implementation problems.

  3. Know whether it works.

    Establish evaluation criteria and performance benchmarks that provide meaningful evidence of effectiveness.

  4. Keep it working.

    Maintain organizational ownership, monitoring, records, continuity, and the ability to respond to change.

  5. Demonstrate that it can be trusted.

    Provide evidence that consequential applications are being operated responsibly and that applicable obligations are being addressed.

What this evidence does and does not show

These sources support the premise behind Progressive AI Governance: governance that is sized to risk, introduced early, and adapted over time. None of them tests the racetrack model itself, which is my framework. Some figures are forecasts, and the surveys differ in sample, population, and date.

Questions

Questions worth asking early.

Is this a maturity model we have to climb?

No. The levels describe kinds of AI use, not stages of organizational maturity. An organization normally operates at all three at once: simple rules for everyday use, managed safeguards for consequential applications, and assured controls where the stakes or regulation require them.

Does every application need the highest level?

No, and that is the point. The objective is not to govern every application in the same way. Applying the highest level everywhere slows the work that does not need it.

How do we know which level an application needs?

By looking at consequence, data, autonomy, obligations, and scale. Inventorying what is in use comes first, since you cannot place an application you do not know about.

How does this relate to ISO/IEC 42001, the NIST AI RMF, and the EU AI Act?

They are references the levels draw on. ISO/IEC 42001 is closest to the organizational management layer, the NIST AI RMF informs risk practices, and the EU AI Act helps decide which uses need Stage 3 controls. See ISO/IEC 42001 implementation.

How would this be put in place for us?

Starting from what your organization requires, and in small steps. One common route is fractional AI governance.
