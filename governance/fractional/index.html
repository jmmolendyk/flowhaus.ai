Needs first

The plan comes from your organization, not from a template.

Frameworks such as ISO/IEC 42001, the NIST AI RMF, and the EU AI Act are useful references, but none of them is a plan. A fractional governance plan starts with six questions about your organization, and the answers decide which services you need and how much of them.

  • What AI is in use?

    Tools, agents, and applications, including the ones teams adopted on their own and the ones embedded in vendor products.

  • What are you obligated to do?

    Regulation, sector expectations, customer and partner requirements, and your own commitments.

  • Who is affected if it goes wrong?

    Customers, patients, employees, and decisions that carry real consequence. Higher consequence earns more rigor.

  • What already exists?

    Compliance, security, privacy, quality, and risk practices that governance can extend instead of duplicate.

  • Who can own it?

    The people, time, and authority your organization has for governance today, and what it will need later.

  • Where is AI use heading?

    Planned projects, new vendors, and agents on the way, so governance is in place before use scales.

From requirements to a fractional governance plan

  1. 01

    Listen and take stock

    Conversations with leadership and the teams using AI, plus a first inventory of what is actually in use.

  2. 02

    Identify what the organization requires

    Translate your obligations, risks, and existing practices into a short, plain list of governance requirements.

  3. 03

    Prioritize the biggest risks and liabilities

    Start where consequence is highest. Everything else is sequenced behind it rather than attempted at once.

  4. 04

    Write the fractional governance plan

    Agree on which services you need, how often I am involved, who owns what, and how progress is reported to leadership.

  5. 05

    Operate, review, and adjust

    Work the plan, review it with leadership on a regular cadence, and change it as your AI use and risk change.

What the plan contains

  • PrioritiesThe risks and liabilities addressed first, and why.
  • Selected servicesOnly the services your requirements call for, drawn from the list below.
  • Cadence and time commitmentHow often we meet and how much of my time the plan assumes. It can scale up or down.
  • Decision rights and ownersWho in your organization approves, who is accountable, and where I advise rather than decide.
  • Reporting to leadershipWhat leadership sees, how often, and what decisions it needs to make.
  • Path to ownershipWhat your team takes over, in what order, and what “done” looks like for my involvement.

Services

Five services. You choose the ones you need.

Each is described as a role in governance rather than a product. The examples show what the work can include; your plan decides what it does include.

  1. Oversight

    Governance that fits how your organization already works, with someone accountable for keeping it moving.

    Can include: setting up or supporting an AI governance committee, a review path for new AI use cases, an AI inventory, and a record of governance decisions.

  2. Regulation

    Reviewing how coming regulation and expected practice affect your particular AI use, so you plan for what applies to you.

    Can include: mapping your AI use to the EU AI Act, NIST AI RMF, or sector guidance, and summarizing what changes and when.

  3. Monitoring

    Performance and risk monitoring turned into summaries leadership can read and act on.

    Can include: defining what to measure, reviewing evaluation results with the owning teams, and regular leadership summaries.

  4. Guidance

    Practical answers as your AI use and risk change, so teams are not left to interpret policy alone.

    Can include: policies and playbooks, reviews of vendor tools and agents, risk and impact assessments, and training for the people who use and build AI.

  5. Ownership

    Continued support toward long-term ownership, and a deliberate transition to your organization.

    Can include: naming internal owners, documenting the program so it runs without me, and a handoff plan, which may be a new hire or an existing role.

Some needs are better met as a defined project than as an ongoing relationship. See focused services for those, including ISO/IEC 42001 implementation.

Methodology

Implemented in small steps, and improved each time around.

Governance that arrives all at once tends to get worked around. I start with one system or one issue, tailor governance to what we learn, and expand outward gradually. It is the same idea as Progressive AI Governance: rigor in proportion to consequence.

  1. 1

    Pilot

    Choose one AI system or one issue, ideally one with real consequence, and apply governance to it end to end.

  2. 2

    Learn

    See what was useful, what created friction, and what the organization actually needed. Adjust before going wider.

  3. 3

    Expand

    Extend to the next systems and teams in order of risk, reusing what worked and tailoring what did not.

  4. 4

    Hand over

    As practices stabilize, move ownership to your people, with documentation and a clear way to keep improving.

The loop repeats as AI use changes. Each pass is reviewed with leadership, so the plan stays matched to what the organization needs.

What the data does not tell us

I could not find a reliable count of organizations using fractional AI governance specifically, and I would be wary of one. Fractional leadership overall is growing, and AI governance needs are growing, but the two have not been measured together. Compensation figures are medians across roles and levels, not a benchmark for a governance officer. The sources also differ in sample, population, and date, so read them as direction rather than precision.

Questions

Questions worth asking early.

How is this different from a consulting project?

A project ends with a deliverable. Fractional governance is ongoing involvement in oversight, review, and guidance while your organization builds its own capability. Some needs fit a project better, and the first conversation is partly about which is which.

How much time does it take, and what does it cost?

There is no standard package. The plan sets the cadence and time commitment from your AI use, risk, and obligations, and it can scale up or down as those change.

When should we move to a full-time hire?

When governance work becomes steady enough, or broad enough, to need a dedicated person or team. The plan names a path to ownership from the start, so that move is a decision you make deliberately rather than a gap you discover.

Do you provide legal advice?

No. I bring governance expertise and program leadership. Questions that need legal interpretation go to your counsel, and I’ll flag when one comes up.

Can fractional governance lead to certification?

It can. If ISO/IEC 42001 certification becomes the right goal, the governance built along the way is the foundation for it. See ISO/IEC 42001 implementation. An accredited body, not me, does the certifying.
