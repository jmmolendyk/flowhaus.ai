Process-first AI

Fix the process, then put AI in it, with people where they matter.

AI creates value when the work around it is redesigned, not when it is added on top. This is how I combine process optimization, business re-engineering with AI, and human-centered design, and how I set boundaries and oversight for AI agents so people stay in control as the work changes.

This is process and design work, not legal advice or a security assessment. Where a question needs counsel or a security specialist, I’ll say so.

Principle

Optimize, then automate.

It comes from my Six Sigma work. Automating a process you have not examined makes its problems faster and harder to see. Three habits follow from it.

  • Understand the work first

    How it actually runs today, where it stalls, and what “better” would be measured by.

  • Design for the people in it

    The people doing, reviewing, and relying on the work shape what the new process should be.

  • Build in control, in proportion

    Limits, checkpoints, and monitoring sized to the consequence of what the AI can do.

Process optimization

Start with the process, not the tool.

Before deciding where AI belongs, I work through the process itself. Many of the gains come from this step, and it makes the AI step far easier to specify and to control.

  1. 01

    Define and measure

    Name the process, its owner, and a baseline: volume, cycle time, error and rework rates, cost, and what customers or staff experience.

  2. 02

    Map how it really works

    Walk the work with the people who do it, including workarounds, handoffs, and exceptions that never made it into the documentation.

  3. 03

    Remove what should not exist

    Eliminate waste, duplicate approvals, and rework before anything is automated. Automating a step that should not exist only makes it permanent.

  4. 04

    Decide what AI should and should not do

    Match each step to a person, a conventional system, or AI, based on consequence, variability, and how checkable the result is.

  5. 05

    Pilot, measure, and improve

    Run the redesigned process on a small scope, compare against the baseline, and adjust before expanding.

Business re-engineering with AI

Redesign the work, not just the workflow.

Dropping an assistant into an existing process usually makes the old work faster. Re-engineering asks what the work should be now that AI can do parts of it, and changes roles, decision rights, and approvals to match.

  1. Divide the work

    Decide which steps AI drafts, which it decides, and which stay with people, and make the handoffs between them explicit.

  2. Set decision rights

    Define who approves what, at what threshold, and who is accountable when an AI-assisted decision goes wrong.

  3. Design the exceptions

    Plan for what happens when the AI is unsure, wrong, or unavailable: who is alerted, what pauses, and how work continues.

  4. Choose the measures

    Track outcomes, quality, and the health of oversight, not only speed or volume.

Human-centered design

Design the human part as carefully as the AI part.

“A human in the loop” is only a control if the person can actually do the job. That depends on design: what they see, how much time they have, and what they are able to change. My graduate training is in human-computer interaction and design, and the discipline applies directly here. It is described in ISO 9241-210, the standard for human-centred design of interactive systems.

  • Understand the context of useWho reviews AI output, under what workload and time pressure, and with what expertise.
  • Make the AI’s work visibleShow sources, confidence, and what the AI did or skipped, in terms the reviewer can use. Explainability is a design goal to test, not a feature to assume.
  • Design against over-reliancePeople tend to trust capable systems too readily. Reviews need to be built to counter that, not to rely on vigilance.
  • Give real authority to overrideA reviewer who cannot easily reject, correct, or stop the AI is not providing oversight.
  • Test with the people who will use itEvaluate the review step with real reviewers and real cases before relying on it.
  • Keep skills aliveIf people only approve, their ability to judge declines. Plan for training and periodic hands-on work.

Agent boundaries and control

The more an agent can do, the more its limits matter.

An agent that can plan and act across systems turns oversight into a design problem. Singapore’s Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI and OWASP’s guidance on excessive agency point to the same starting place: bound what the agent can do before deciding how humans oversee it.

Bound it by design

  • Least functionalityGive the agent only the tools the task needs. OWASP names excessive functionality, permissions, and autonomy as root causes of agent harm.
  • Least privilegeGrant minimum access, with its own identity, never more authority than the person who authorized it. Enforce limits in the system, not only in prompts.
  • Defined scopeSay what the agent may do, with which data, and what it must hand off. Follow defined procedures where unpredictability would be costly.
  • Approval checkpointsRequire a person to approve high-stakes, irreversible, or unusual actions such as payments or deletions, and deny by default if the approval step fails.
  • A way to stop itClear ability to pause, roll back, or shut an agent down, with someone named who can do it.
  • Accountable ownershipEvery agent has a named owner. Responsibility stays with people and the organization, even when vendors and platforms are involved.

Choose the level of human involvement

These terms are used loosely, so here is how I use them. The right level depends on consequence, not on how impressive the agent is, and one agent can use different levels for different actions.

  1. 1

    In the loop

    A person approves each consequential action before it happens. Suited to high-stakes or irreversible steps.

  2. 2

    On the loop

    The agent acts within set limits while a person monitors, samples, and can intervene. Suited to moderate consequence at volume.

  3. 3

    Within bounds

    Low-consequence, reversible actions run inside hard limits, with periodic review of results. Never unbounded.

Moving an action to a lighter level is a decision with an owner, evidence from the pilot, and a review date.

Monitoring and review

As agents scale, the human work becomes monitoring and review.

Watching every step of every agent does not scale, and constant approval requests lead to alert fatigue. The Singapore framework pairs targeted checkpoints with automated monitoring and alerts. That makes monitoring and review a job to design, staff, and measure in its own right.

  • Track the reviewers, not only the agentThe framework suggests watching how often humans reject agent actions and how quickly they review. Very low rejection or very fast reviews can signal rubber-stamping.
  • Alert on what mattersAutomated monitoring for anomalies, limit breaches, and unusual behavior, tuned so that alerts stay meaningful.
  • Make requests short and clearApproval requests should state the action and its risk level in a few lines a reviewer can act on.
  • Sample and auditReview a rotating sample of approved and automated actions, so oversight does not depend on the agent’s own reporting.
  • Roll out graduallyWiden an agent’s scope in stages, with each stage reviewed against the pilot measures.
  • Feed it back into the processRejections, overrides, and incidents show where the process or the agent’s limits need to change.

Governance, policy, and ISO/IEC 42001 work build on these controls. See AI governance.

What this evidence does and does not show

The surveys link process redesign and defined human validation with better results, but they cannot show that one causes the other, and the respondents are mostly large organizations. Research on human oversight is also a caution: Ben Green’s review of 41 policies found evidence that people often cannot perform the oversight roles assigned to them. That is why this page treats oversight as something to design and test, not assume.

Questions

Questions worth asking early.

Do we need to redesign the process before using AI?

Not always, and not all at once. For low-consequence uses, a light review may be enough. For work that touches customers, money, or regulated decisions, understanding and improving the process first usually pays back in a simpler, safer AI step.

Is a human in the loop enough?

Only if the person can do the job. Reviewers need the right information, time, and authority, and the review needs testing and monitoring. Otherwise it can become a formality.

When can an agent act without approval?

When its actions are low in consequence, reversible, and inside hard limits, and when you have pilot evidence and ongoing monitoring. The decision should have an owner and a review date.

Do you build the agents?

No. I design the process, boundaries, and oversight, and work with your engineers or vendors who build. If a project needs engineering or legal work, I’ll tell you what I’d recommend and refer you to independent firms.

How does this relate to governance?
